# Method guide — reading dated and conflicting scouting reports

*Pivot Scouting Methods, Methods desk (fictional) · 2025-10-01 · method guide*

Scouting reports on the same player often disagree, and some reports in Pivot's files are seasons old. This method guide sets out how to weigh dated and conflicting scouting reports. The short version: prefer recent reports and larger samples, but always record the disagreement instead of hiding it.

## Prefer recent reports and larger samples
When two scouting reports conflict, start with date and sample size. The more recent report usually describes the player as he is now, and a report built on months of film outweighs one built on a few early-season weeks, when roles, opponents and conditioning are still settling. Preferring the newer report does not mean deleting the older one: keep both, cite both, and say which one a conclusion leans on and why.

## Consider the analyst's vantage
Conflicting scouting reports often come from analysts looking from different places. Film scouts watch possessions: screen navigation, effort, communication. Data analysts see what the stat sheet records and know what it cannot. Video coordinators see how opponents game-planned a player. Each vantage sees part of the player. Before choosing between a film report and a data note, ask what each analyst could and could not observe, and which view fits the question being asked.

## Age-related decline makes old reports history
Scouting reports age along with the players they describe. An evaluation written two or three seasons ago may predate age-related decline in lateral quickness, recovery speed or availability. For veterans past thirty, treat an evaluation more than a season old as history rather than a current grade. The older report still shows what the player was and how far he has moved; it should not set his current evaluation.

## Never average grades into a fake consensus
Two conflicting grades are information, not noise to average away. Splitting the difference between a strong grade and a weak one produces a middling grade that no analyst gave and that describes no real player. Report the conflict explicitly: who said what, when, and on what sample. Then name what would resolve it: more film from a later stretch of the season, playoff possessions against a real game plan, or a focused look at the disputed skill.

# Method guide — positional defense vs team defense

*Pivot Scouting Methods, Methods desk (fictional) · 2025-10-01 · method guide*

Scouting reports in this corpus grade defense on three separate dimensions: point-of-attack, positional or wing, and team or help defense. This guide defines each and explains why a player can be elite in one and average in another. It also warns against reading steals as defense and assuming regular-season defense carries into the playoffs.

## Point-of-attack defense

Point-of-attack defense is containing the ball handler at the start of a possession. A strong point-of-attack defender stays in front, navigates screens by going over, under or through them, pressures the ball without fouling and keeps the handler out of the middle. Point-of-attack failures show up as downhill drives that force a second defender to help, which is where many team-defense breakdowns begin.

## Positional and wing defense

Positional or wing defense is guarding bigger wings one-on-one and switching across positions. It rewards length, strength and balance: absorbing contact and staying attached after a switch. A defender who excels at positional defense against bigger wings can still struggle at the point of attack against small, quick guards, and the reverse is common. Reports should grade the two separately.

## Team and help defense

Team or help defense is defending as part of five: rotating on time, tagging the roller, closing out under control, and communicating screens and switches. A player can be elite on the ball and still be a tick late on rotations, or an average individual defender who lifts a unit by talking and being in position. Team-defense grades need off-ball film.

## Steals are a noisy proxy for defense

Steals measure one outcome, not defensive quality. A high steal total can come from gambling in passing lanes, which leaves teammates covering; a disciplined point-of-attack defender may record few steals because he keeps the ball handler in front instead of reaching. Use steals as a prompt to watch film, never as a defensive grade.

## Playoff possessions stress the point of attack

Playoff possessions stress point-of-attack defense differently from the regular season. Opponents slow down, run more half-court sets and use repeated screens to hunt the weakest on-ball defender. A defender who holds up in the regular season may be targeted on every possession of a series. Because this corpus holds no playoff statistics, playoff judgments rest on qualitative film notes.
